## Artisan

There's a common sentiment that engineers will go the way of the artisan. I think even that analogy is too optimistic; at least the word "artisanal" connotes a level of quality and care above that of mass produced, commoditized objects.

Instead, it seems to me that we truly will get to live in an abundant world where there will be no compromises between quality and quantity. I am very excited about this world -- it makes sense that we should strive for a better and more beautiful future where no human is constrained by the reality or perception that we live under scarcity. This is a belief I hold stronglt. 

As I have become more convinced that this future is imminent, I think it necessarily follows that **engineers** and (speculatively) everyone **technical** will become functionally obsolete. And because it's important to keep definitions clear, I define **technical** to mean anyone with specific, expert knowledge in a subject in order to accomplish some task. It's a broader definition than most would use, but I like how faithful it is to the etymology of the word 'technology'. 

This "reckoning" of humanity's pursuit towards innovation feels inevitable. As humans became more and more productive, it makes sense that we would eventually come to eliminate drudgery entirely. Progress has always been motivated to, at some level, reduce unnecessary work and effort, even if reality has caused all previous and existing systems of allocation to fail partly (and some more drastically)[^econ]. Taken to its conclusion, becoming technical or an expert, to achieve some end is itself, in an ideal world, drudgery.

[^econ]: kept vague because idgaf about talking economic theory rn

Can you imagine a world where we built global internet coverage with just a snap of the fingers? No decades of research and engineering and protocol after protocol, no undersea optical cables, no rocket scientists and no rocket engineers. I think it's inarguable that such a world would be better than ours. 

I've been struggling to understand what this future may look like. I've been struggling to understand to what extent the purpose of living is. And I've been starting drafts and writing down little snippets for far too long over the past year. So these are finally some of my thoughts in a mildly more coherent, written form. 

## Impotent

I started seriously thinking about abundance and more explicitly AI somewhere near the start of 2026. I was impressed sometime around April when, using AI, I as someone non-technical with respect to FPGAs and embedded systems built a [maximally resource utilizing rasterizer for Minecraft](https://github.com/WT-MM/embedded-block-game) on the De1-SoC platform[^de1soc].

[^de1soc]: an educational hardware development board with an FPGA

However, the inflection point happened for me somewhere around the end of Summer 2026 (say late August, early September). I think that's when Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra came out. A few weeks before that, it already seemed to me that a lot of software engineering was over. At work, I never actually wrote code. Instead I was trying to understand tradeoffs, absorb as much domain knowledge relevant to whatever problem I was solving at the time was, and trying desperately along with everyone else to adapt to the new form of engineering. 

When Astra came out, it was shockingly proficient at using the computer and browser just as a human would. I had it design various parts for me in OnShape (the CAD tool), undergo all of the random administrative burdens of starting another year at university, and of course build a lot of useful software including the editor that I'm now writing this in.

In other words, I'd begun to feel quite **impotent**. Keeping up with the definitions, here I'm referring to *impotens*, as in "without power". It wasn't that I couldn't get things done (I could get more done than ever), but rather that it was no longer a differentiator to do things. It felt like my own ability did not matter much anymore.

### Three Futilities

Throughout my life, I had come to view futility as mainly coming in two flavors: the futility of impermanence and the futility of insignificance. I'd mostly come to terms and settled on an almost zen mindset with regards to the two. However, I've realized that there's a third flavor of futility, which is that of redundancy. 

Impermanence brings to mind, maybe oddly in order, 2b2t[^2b2t], Burning Man, 4chan, and of course sand mandalas. They're all examples of places where things get built despite knowing at the end they'll be destroyed. Humans and philosophers have talked and written at length, since we all know at the end life is finite and whatever we leave behind will eventually be ground and dispersed into the dirt. The general takeaway I had was that nothing I make will last, yet I still find joy in making it and in the brief moment after it's completed but not yet gone.

[^2b2t]: an anarchist Minecraft server

Futility of insignificance is also a well tread idea. Many of my own thoughts come from the various existentialist philosophers I read when I was younger. In short, nothing we do matters from a cosmic perspective. The whole pale blue dot, endless expanse of the night sky, yada yada. The problem is that nobody actually lives and cares about that zoomed out perspective unless you're an angsty teenager. When you wake up, you still think about what food you're gonna eat, what random problems you'll have during work, who you'll interact with that day[^nagel].

[^nagel]: I yoinked this from Thomas Nagel's in "The Absurd" among other things. Absurdity of reality comes from our ability to step back, not from the universe itself.

Because those other two futilities are something I've already mulled over, this third new flavor of futility has hit much harder than I expected. **Redundancy** is the question of whether it needed to be *me*. Whether it matters at all that *I* do anything. This is almost a question that I guess I never stopped to think about because I'd always managed to be good enough[^betterthanyou]. Even as I've maintained a feeling of definite inferiority to the many talented people I've been lucky enough to interact with throughout my life, I still manage to hold a dissonant idea that I can be greater.

[^betterthanyou]: honestly I don't think I've ever not been in some way arrogant / egotistical. 

Now though, it turns that it actually didn't matter that I spent years learning and working because all of a sudden *expertise doesn't matter*. Right now, I can't stop thinking about the Minecraft rasterizer. It took me a good few weeks back in April, and it was one of the most purely joyful things I'd done or built during that time. But I'm pretty sure anyone else could do it in about thirty minutes now. I can only imagine what someone who spent decades working on FPGAs would feel (though I did get a sense for it during an extended chat with my Embedded Systems professor over the summer). 

So I think redundancy is mostly just **insignificance made empirical**. I think these three futilities all ultimately ask: "did it make a difference?"

To which the answer has always been no. They ask this question across time, across space and scale, and across people (a world without me). I thought I'd managed to deal with this question but I'd just been deluding myself because they were so abstract. With AI though, futility became very in-my-face. Impossible to ignore. 

## Cheater

I tried to figure out why I was struggling so much more to define meaning. The first thought that was something about authentic difficulty: I don't want to live in a world where we go to physical and mental gyms and social gyms and whatever other constructs arise because nothing needs to be done anymore.

I don't think that's really true, since I still enjoy imposed difficulties -- I love playing board and card games after all. 

I think thematically it's similar to cheating. By definition, playing a game almost means taking on unnecessary restrictions. If you cheat, you're not really playing the game and if everyone cheats, there's not much point at all in playing. 

Going back to the artisans and engineering and expertise, the reality is that engineering was never a game. The rules were set by necessity because there was no other way to do it. As AI becomes better, that necessity begins to go away, and the strict rules that I and we all played with begins to crumble faster and faster. I felt a bit lost because the game I enjoyed so much was disappearing. 

This helped me to realize I was living a more inauthentic life than I knew. I enjoyed being good (or maybe just the illusion of being good) at what I did because I got some level of satisfaction from being better than others. Ironically though, in losing the inauthentic value of engineering, I've come to care more about what it is I actually do in this world. That I do intrinsically care about solving problems and making the world better. Insofar as expertise *to achieve some end*  is drudgery, I hadn't known which parts of my expertise were a means to an end and which parts were The Thing Itself until AI came to pull them apart. 

## Hopeless Optimist

Existence before essence -- those philosophers were probably onto something. I've come to agree generally that we are things in this universe that create their own meaning. This meaninglessness and despair and AI doomerism aren't anything new, but they have showed me that rationally understanding "impotence" or "angst"[^kierkegaard] as the fear and the dizziness of the infinite don't actually help you once you're confronted with it. 

[^kierkegaard]: need to tie in kierkegaard somehow, and yes i pronounce it /ɑŋst/

I took Principles of Economics[^principles] last semester, so I'm more or less caught up on what these economists are up to. Keynes predicted in 1930 that by around 2030 the "economic problem" would be solved and humanity would face the real, permanent problem of living  "wisely and agreeably and well." He thought there'd be a collective panic along the way as people lost the structure of the work that had supported their lives. This is probably true and will continue to be more true as more people come to the same existential panic that I have. 

At the same time, I've also come to adopt a newfound faith. I really don't think we can know what the future will actually look like and what the experience of being redundant will do to us. There's no point in worrying about post-scarcity because it might just be impossible to understand what it'll be like and for now, we still need to get there. The "me" who has to deal with that might be entirely alien, so I just have faith that it will all work out in the end.

I've come back around to being optimistic again. We haven't "won" life yet. The world still isn't utopia, there are still real problems and real needs in the world and real meaning in getting us from here and now to then and there.

Right now we are in the most important game not just of our lives but of all human history, and luckily for us, *it still won't be a game* for a tiny bit longer. When it does all end, I know that it will have been the right thing to work towards and I have the faith that I'll find life worth living in the new world.

When I was a kid maybe in 3rd grade or something like that, I used to play a lot with these flat geometric blocks after school. I'd make a whole 2D world -- my own Flatland -- filled with cathedrals and cars, boats and trees. Every few days, when I'd wait in the classroom for my brother and sister to come back from their after school clubs, I'd play with foam blocks to make castles and cities.

At the end of every day, we would terrorize those cities. The three of us, stomping around, destroying all the work I'd done. I think those days were when I first knew I wanted to make something in this world. I interpreted that innate desire as a need to be an engineer, but I don’t think it was ever about being an engineer.
